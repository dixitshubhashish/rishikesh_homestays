City overview

Rishikesh: a river town of pilgrims, yogis, and adventure seekers.

A holy town on the Ganga, a global yoga destination, and the gateway to the Garhwal Himalayas — Rishikesh has always been several places at once. Understanding its layers helps you pick the right area to stay.

A sacred town on the Ganga

Rishikesh sits where the Ganga leaves the Himalayan foothills and meets the plains, a geography that has made it a natural pilgrimage point for centuries. Devotional tradition connects the name itself to Lord Vishnu — "Hrishikesh" — said to have appeared here to a sage after long austerities, and older accounts also refer to the area as "Kubjamrak." The Mahabharata and Ramayana both mention the region, and the Raghunath Temple, with its Rishikund tank, is tied to legends of Rama himself. Adi Shankaracharya's visit in the 9th century CE is often credited with lifting Rishikesh's standing as a pilgrimage centre, cementing its role as a starting point for the Char Dham routes into the mountains.

From pilgrim town to the world's yoga capital

Rishikesh was a domestic Hindu pilgrimage destination long before it had any international profile. That changed in February 1968, when The Beatles travelled here for an extended meditation course at Maharishi Mahesh Yogi's ashram — the site now widely known as the Beatles Ashram, inside what is formally the Chaurasi Kutia complex. The band wrote dozens of songs during their stay, several of which appeared on the White Album, and the global attention that followed is commonly credited as the turning point that put Rishikesh on the international yoga and meditation map. The town has built on that ever since, with a large concentration of yoga ashrams, teacher-training schools, and an annual International Yoga Festival.

Rivers, rapids, and a newer adventure identity

Alongside its spiritual reputation, Rishikesh gradually became North India's main adventure-sports base — white-water rafting on the Ganga, camping, trekking, bungee jumping, and more. This grew over decades rather than around a single moment, and today it shapes the daily rhythm of areas like Tapovan as much as the temples and ashrams do. The result is a town where sadhus, long-term yoga practitioners, pilgrim families, and backpackers all share the same ghats — sometimes within the same afternoon.

Rishikesh and Haridwar: two towns, one river

About 25 km downstream sits Haridwar, one of Hinduism's seven most sacred cities and a very different experience from Rishikesh. Where Rishikesh blends yoga, wellness, and adventure with its spirituality, Haridwar is older and more ritual-dense — its evening Ganga Aarti at Har Ki Pauri, run by hereditary priest families at the Brahma Kund, is louder, larger, and more traditional than Rishikesh's own aarti at Parmarth Niketan. The short distance between the two makes a combined visit a common itinerary: ritual immersion in Haridwar, calmer riverside days in Rishikesh.

Haridwar's Kumbh Mela and the road ahead to 2027

Haridwar's next major Kumbh Mela gathering is expected in 2027, following the traditional roughly-twelve-year cycle. Rishikesh, a short drive away, is a natural, calmer base for travellers who want to experience it without competing for lodging in Haridwar itself. Traveller tips

  • Rishikesh is a holy town: nearly all restaurants are vegetarian and alcohol-free, and this is worth planning around rather than working against.
  • Ask your homestay about stairs, parking, luggage help, and vehicle access before arrival.
  • Traffic can slow down near bridges and popular ghats during evenings, weekends, and holidays.
  • For quiet sleep, ask whether the stay is near a main road, cafe strip, temple loudspeaker, or event area.
